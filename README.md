### Bezier Clipped Metaballs

_Metaballs_ is a classic rendering technique known for making blobby spheres. It works by treating points in space as having a charge with a falloff- literally, simulating an electromagnetic field- and then finding the "isosurface" around that point, which is just the region where the value of the field reaches a certain threshold. For a single charge, this is just a fancy way of rendering a sphere, but it gets interesting when you start adding multiple metaballs into the mix. The charged fields overlap additively, creating the distinctive blobby look of metaballs. While initially used to render electron fields in early visualizations of molecules, it's since found a wide range of applications, notably, video games.

When my team and I were building [Soar: Pillars of Tasneem](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1513030/Soar_Pillars_of_Tasneem/), we needed an unsettling, out-of-place effect for corruption, and we settled on metaballs as the basis for that look. _Soar_ is low poly, sharp edges and hard lines, something soft and organic would stick out like a sore thumb. However, performance was a problem- due to our time and budget constraints, I was forced to use raymarched rendering, which is slow. Luckily, modern graphics cards are powerful, and, with a lot of help from some very smart people, I was able to optimize it reasonably well. Reasonably is the key word here, a lot of compromises were made, and I never felt satisfied with that implementation.

During my research, I discovered Nishita Lab's seminal work on metaballs, dating back to the 90s. Miraculously, all of their work is publicly available, and I included the relevant snippet of their 1990 paper in this repository. Their approach is as follows: if we shoot a ray through a charged field, the density of that field is guaranteed to be a degree-6 polynomial. While it's been proven that there is no "hexatic equation" that can allow us to solve for some threshold value algebraically, we can easily generate Bezier points that perfectly represent that curve. As it turns out, you can iteratively approximate the X value of any given Y- allowing you to find where the original polynomial (and thus, the charged field itself) reach a certain threshold value that then forms the metaball isosurface. along a Bezier curve using a process called Bezier clipping, which can get good results in fewer than 4 iterations.

Implementing Bezier clipped metaball rendering is trivial on the CPU, but quite challenging on the GPU, and even moreso within the limits of Unreal Engine. I began work on it during _Soar_'s development; I wrote a python script to demonstrate my understanding, and then tried to port that to shader code. I made some progress, but stalled out, and dropped it to focus on more critical tasks. I never forgot about it though, and, about a year after launch, I found myself with some free time and decided to pick it up again. Just like before, I quickly got stuck, but this time I decided to try an LLM first workflow. I experimented with a variety of models, and ultimately settled on Opus 5 as the best of both cost and performance. I went back to my original python script, and had it generate a visualizer & validate the algorithm. Then, I had it translate the code to HLSL, tested and optimized it by hand, and finally settled on a roughly 2x performance improvement over raymarching- which I am more than happy with.

To test the visualizer yourself, make sure you're running Python 3.10 and run either bezier_launcher.bat (Windows) or bezier_launcher.sh (everyone else). To see the shader in action, you can download my [Fab Asset](https://www.fab.com/listings/60938342-46ef-4c04-8c9d-7a502af73391?tab=reviews), or see it running in _Soar_. I've also included a harness and handoff for easy future development or code analysis.
